*Security Architecture · Published October 11, 2026*

Imagine entering a school. Being inside the building does not automatically give you permission to open every room.

**Zero Trust is a security approach that avoids automatically trusting access just because someone is inside a network or using a company-owned device.**

Instead, systems use identity, permissions, device condition, and other relevant signals to decide whether to allow access.

## Three useful principles
- **Verify explicitly:** use evidence to make the access decision.
- **Use least privilege:** allow only the access needed.
- **Assume breach:** plan for an account or device to be compromised and limit the possible damage.

## A simple example
An employee signs in to open a work file. A policy checks the employee’s identity, permission to read the file, and relevant device or risk signals.

The result might be access, an additional verification step, or a denial. Being on the office Wi-Fi is not enough by itself.

Systems can reassess access as conditions change. This does not mean a person must type a password for every click.

## Try it on paper
Compare two requests for the same private file: an authorized employee using an approved device, and someone using a stolen account on an unknown device. What evidence would help you decide?

**Remember: Zero Trust is an approach supported by many controls, rather than a single product.**

## 60–90 second video
Start with school doors inside a building. Show identity, permission, and device evidence going into a decision. End with allow, extra checks, or deny. Explain that location alone does not establish trust.
Suggested pacing: 10 seconds for the everyday analogy, 25 seconds for the concept, 25 seconds for the access example, and 10 seconds for the takeaway.
Use large captions and slow speech. Explain acronyms before using them. Keep analogy scenes separate from computer-system scenes.

## Paper lab
Use fictional identity, permission, and device cards. Make a simple rule: a known authorized learner with an acceptable device can read their course; missing evidence needs more checks; no course permission means deny. Change one signal and explain the new decision. This illustrates policy decisions, not a complete Zero Trust architecture.
Record the request, relevant evidence, decision, and reason. No accounts, configuration changes, or paid software are needed.
